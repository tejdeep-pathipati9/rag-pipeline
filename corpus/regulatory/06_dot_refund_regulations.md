# U.S. DOT Refund Rights — Attributed Research Snapshot

## 1. Purpose and authority
The DOT page describes U.S. airline passenger refund rights and the relationship between significant disruptions, substitute travel, and acceptance or refusal. DOT requirements have a different source of authority from airline-specific goodwill commitments. A private airline may promise hotels or meals in addition to what federal law requires, but a service commitment must not be misquoted as a federal refund statute. FlightResolve uses this digest only for domestic itineraries in its pilot dataset; international rules are noted for context and excluded from automated resolution.

## 2. When a flight change qualifies for a refund
### 2.1 Canceled service
A covered flight cancellation may entitle a passenger to refund of an unused ticket when the traveler chooses not to proceed or take replacement transportation or alternative compensation. The reason for cancellation does not generally remove this basic refund right. A canceled flight is therefore a different trigger than an airline's discretionary hotel commitment.

### 2.2 Significant change on domestic travel
DOT lists criteria that include a domestic itinerary scheduled to depart its **origin** at least three hours earlier, or scheduled to arrive at its **final destination** at least three hours later, than the originally scheduled itinerary. Other listed circumstances include a different origin or destination airport, an itinerary with more connections, and an involuntary cabin downgrade. Certain disability-related accessibility changes have additional significance criteria. The three-hour comparison must not be carelessly applied to a single incoming segment if it is the change in final arrival that the scenario asks about.

### 2.3 Passenger travel decision
The refund right for significant disruption depends on whether the passenger accepts the changed travel, replacement transportation, credits, or other alternatives and actually travels. A person who takes the substituted flight ordinarily cannot also demand the entire airfare back under the significant-change provision alone. Other partial refunds, including downgrade differences and unused services, should be evaluated on their own terms.

## 3. Alternative offers and refunds
### 3.1 Airline rebooking is not the federal refund mechanism
DOT explains that airlines frequently offer substitute flights, while rebooking commitments are also described in carrier service plans. An airline's willingness to rebook does not itself decide whether a consumer qualifies for a refund. The customer must be told about an applicable refund right when alternative transportation or compensation is offered.

### 3.2 Declining an alternative
If a qualifying traveler rejects alternative travel or compensation, an automatic refund may be owed without requiring a separate application. If the passenger says "I might accept," this is not the same as rejection. Refund workflows should capture the actual choice and notification evidence.

### 3.3 No response and departure timing
DOT guidance distinguishes explicit rejection from a nonresponse. When an offer is not answered and the passenger does not take the alternative, the refund trigger and deadline depend in part on the relevant flight or substitute flight departure. Do not mark an open and unexpired offer as "refunded" merely because its recipient has not yet replied.

### 3.4 Timing and method
The DOT consumer guidance describes refunds to the original form of payment, with **seven business days for credit-card purchases** and **twenty calendar days for certain other payments** following the applicable trigger. Operational implementations must use the exact trigger event and payment method; an answer giving an exact payment date without either field is unsupported. For tickets sold by a ticket agent, the merchant of record may be responsible for the airfare refund.

## 4. Common misunderstandings
| Claim | Research assessment |
|---|---|
| "A weather delay can never lead to a refund" | Incorrect: reason for a cancellation or qualifying change is not the same as hotel controllability |
| "Any missed connection earns a cash refund" | Incorrect: analyze disruption significance, passenger response, and unused transportation |
| "Three-hour inbound delay always means three-hour arrival change" | Incorrect: evaluate itinerary-level final arrival |
| "Accepting and flying the new itinerary still yields full airfare refund" | Generally incorrect under significant-change refund rationale |
| "A hotel is federally guaranteed after every overnight delay" | Incorrect as a generalization; distinguish airline commitments from federal ticket refunds |
